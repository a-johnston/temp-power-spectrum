We want to resolve a specific physical question:

**Can nonlinear evolution of exclusively short-wavelength primordial perturbations produce a physical long-wavelength white-noise contribution in a radiation-only universe, and does that contribution survive in an operationally defined observable?**

Distinguish three possible outcomes: an infrared-enhanced response, a finite white-noise contribution, and a response suppressed by positive powers of the output wavenumber. Showing that a \(1/K^2\) pole cancels does not distinguish the latter two.

### Model and definitions

Use spatially flat general relativity with a single perfect radiation fluid, \(p=\rho/3\), and smooth evolution over a fixed finite time interval.

Choose the regular growing solution with nonlinear primordial curvature \(\zeta_{\rm nl}=\epsilon\zeta_1\), where \(\zeta_1\) is Gaussian. Its spectrum must be smooth and supported in a finite band \(q_{\min}<q<q_{\max}\), with \(q_{\min}>0\). There are no independent primordial higher-order scalar, vector, or tensor modes. Retain all nonlinear initial metric and fluid perturbations required by the constraints.

Use a precisely stated nonlinear definition of the comoving curvature perturbation \(\mathcal R\), including slicing and spatial threading. Explain its geometric meaning. Keep this definition fixed throughout.

The final operational target is the observed temperature anisotropy: isotropic emission in the fluid frame on \(T=T_*\), observation by a comoving observer at fixed proper time after crossing a specified initial-temperature surface, and normalization by that observer’s measured sky mean. Remove the dipole after normalization.

“White noise” means a nonzero constant limit of the **dimensional spatial power spectrum**, \(P_X(K)\to A_0>0\). Distinguish this from dimensionless power and from an angular \(C_\ell\).

### First route: the smallest decisive calculation

Study two short modes whose wavevectors nearly cancel. A useful deterministic seed is
\[
\zeta_1(\mathbf x)
=A\cos(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x)
+B\cos[(\mathbf q-\mathbf K)\cdot\mathbf x].
\]

Isolate the quadratic \(AB\) contribution at wavevector \(\mathbf K\). Take \(K/q\to0\) at fixed short scale and fixed finite time, keeping a generic angle between \(\mathbf K\) and \(\mathbf q\). Allow the short modes to be inside the horizon while the output remains outside it.

Derive the leading \(K\)-dependence of the **complete** second-order curvature response. Determine whether singular terms cancel and, crucially, whether a finite \(K^0\) term survives. If it vanishes, find the first surviving power. Include the nonlinear constraints, the required initial data, and all terms completing the chosen curvature variable.

For a symmetric quadratic kernel defined by
\[
X_2(\mathbf K)=
\int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}
F_2(\mathbf q,\mathbf K-\mathbf q)
\zeta_1(\mathbf q)\zeta_1(\mathbf K-\mathbf q),
\]
evaluate the leading behavior of
\[
P_{22}(K)=
2\int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}
P_{\zeta_1}(q)P_{\zeta_1}(|\mathbf K-\mathbf q|)
|F_2(\mathbf q,\mathbf K-\mathbf q)|^2.
\]

An angular average of \(F_2\) that vanishes does not imply that the average of \(|F_2|^2\) vanishes.

For this spatial calculation, explain why the absence of initial long modes eliminates \(P_{13}(K)\) below the initial band. Do not assume the same simplification for the angular spectrum.

Identify separately any long-wavelength contribution already present in the nonlinear initial data or measurement definition, and any contribution produced during subsequent evolution.

### Second route: an exact or all-orders argument

Independently seek an exact conservation or constraint identity that fixes the leading long-wavelength response while retaining short gradients and short-scale nonlinearities.

State precisely what the identity proves: absence of a singular enhancement, absence of a constant white-noise term, or stronger suppression.

If using a conserved nonlinear curvature quantity, derive its relation to the chosen \(\mathcal R\) and to the observable. If coarse-graining the fluid, retain the effective stresses generated by the short modes.

A gradient expansion that makes every mode long does not address this problem. Neither does a consistency relation in which an input mode becomes long.

Distinguish an identity valid order by order in amplitude from a genuinely nonperturbative result. State the order of the \(K\to0\), small-amplitude, and time limits, and identify any nonuniformity.

### Establish the observational meaning

For any surviving leading curvature contribution, demonstrate its effect—or its cancellation—in the complete normalized temperature observable in the same limit. A single explicitly calculated angular temperature difference or quadrupole response is sufficient; a complete angular power spectrum is unnecessary.

Include the emission, observer, and propagation terms required for that test. Distinguish the generated long spatial contribution from direct projection of short modes and from other nonlinear effects along the ray.

White noise in a local composite or in a redefined curvature variable alone does not establish the desired temperature signal. Conversely, removal of an exactly homogeneous mode does not prove that the connected spectrum vanishes as \(K\to0\).

### Required answer

Begin with a short plain-language verdict, stating separately what has been established for the spatial curvature and for the measured temperature.

Provide one of:

1. An explicit nonzero leading coefficient, with its physical interpretation and controlled validity.
2. A suppression theorem with hypotheses that cover the stated setup.
3. A precise unresolved equation or assumption preventing either conclusion.

An unevaluated formal kernel is not the requested endpoint. If numerical or symbolic checks are used, supply the actual code and distinguish checks performed from checks proposed.

Aim for three to five pages of main reasoning. Put only essential supporting calculations in an appendix. Prioritize the decisive coefficient or identity over a catalogue of perturbative terms.
